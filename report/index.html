Is Statistics Canada's data actually being used?

An independent, ongoing test of whether AI search tools and chatbots use Canada's official statistics when they answer everyday questions — or quietly route around them in favour of blogs, private surveys, and other websites.

This is an independent research project, not affiliated with or endorsed by Statistics Canada. Every number below is checked against StatCan's own published tables. Source data, dates, and methodology notes are linked throughout.

How this works

Three simple checks, repeated across dozens of real questions

Statistics Canada publishes huge amounts of accurate, free, official data — on everything from unemployment to loneliness to how many Canadians have a family doctor. But when people ask an AI search tool a question StatCan could answer, does the AI actually reach for it?

For every question in this study, we do the same three things:

1

Ask a real question

The kind people actually type — "how many Canadians have a family doctor," not a technical query.

2

Read the AI's answer

What number did it give, and which website did it credit for that number?

3

Check against StatCan

Does StatCan actually publish this? If so, does the AI's number match, and did it say so?

Read this first: everything below is the “before” picture

Nothing has been changed yet. The experiment's actual test — does making StatCan's tables easier for machines to read change what the AI says? — depends on search engines re-crawling and re-indexing the web, which takes weeks and hasn't happened yet. So the “waves” here are really rounds of baseline measurement: a careful record of what the AI answers today, before any treatment could have had an effect. The follow-up checks (starting August 2026) are what will show whether it made a difference.

Wave 1 · Labour, digital economy, community

The first test: a live before/after experiment

Not one of Bing's 11 answers cited StatCan cleanly — its single “correct” case still mixed in competing, out-of-date numbers. Duck.ai cited StatCan correctly on 6 of 11, and its two worst cases were the same two Bing skipped entirely (charitable donations → the Fraser Institute; trust in government → the OECD) — topics where a rival organization's number has simply won the internet.

This is the pre-treatment baseline. Five of the nine tables behind these gaps are now being made more "crawlable" (plain HTML, clearly labelled, machine-readable) while four similar tables are deliberately left untouched as a control group. We re-check the same 11 questions on a fixed schedule — index check around Aug 2, a full re-check around Aug 30, and the primary result around Oct 11, 2026 — to see whether the change actually moves anything.

Wave 2 · Health, immigration & population

Widening the test

Wave 2 repeats the same three-step check across new subjects. All 25 Health questions and all 25 Immigration questions have now been checked against Bing's AI answers, with Population sampled alongside; Duck.ai (which cites StatCan far more reliably) serves as the comparison surface. As of July 25, 2026, crawlable mirrors of the clearest Health cases are live — so, exactly as in Wave 1, the “after” measurement now waits on the scheduled re-audits. Both waves are live experiments at the same stage: treatment pages published, results pending.

First, a fair-test rule. Of the 25 Health questions, 6 were set aside before scoring because Statistics Canada simply doesn't publish an answer to them — opioid deaths belong to the Public Health Agency, cancer projections to the Canadian Cancer Society. There was nothing of StatCan's for the AI to skip, so scoring them would be unfair to the AI and would water down the result. That leaves 19 questions where StatCan genuinely has the answer — the only ones that can test whether the AI uses it.

Method · audited July 25, 2026

How we're trying not to fool ourselves

It is easy to run a test like this and see whatever you hoped to see. Before any “after” measurements were taken, the whole design was audited for the kinds of mistakes that would quietly wreck the conclusion. Here is what protects the result — and, just as important, what it still can't tell you.

What protects the result

  • A control group. Some tables get a crawlable page; a matched set deliberately gets nothing. If AI answers improve everywhere, that's the industry changing, not us. Only a gap that opens between the two groups counts as an effect.
  • The comparison is fair in both directions. Tables were picked because they looked bad at the start — and things that start unusually bad tend to drift better on their own. Because the control tables were chosen the same way, that drift shows up in both groups and cancels out.
  • The questions were fixed in advance. The wording, the engines, the dates, and what counts as success were all written down before treatment, so nothing can be quietly redefined later to manufacture a win.
  • One headline test, not thirty. With enough measurements, something always looks impressive by luck. A single primary question was named up front: does Bing start citing Statistics Canada on the treated tables but not the untreated ones, by mid-October? Everything else is explicitly secondary.
  • Only fair questions are scored. Questions StatCan doesn't cover are removed before scoring, not counted as failures (see the note in Wave 2).
  • Answers get graded without knowing which group they're in, so a hopeful coder can't nudge borderline calls.

What it can't tell you

  • It's small. Seventeen tables in total. This can demonstrate that something happens and show you real examples; it cannot measure how big the effect is, and there will be no statistics claiming otherwise.
  • AI answers are unstable. Ask the same question twice and you can get different answers — on an ordinary Bing results page, roughly a quarter of the time no AI answer appeared at all (Bing's dedicated AI mode nearly always answers — so that's a quirk of where you ask, not of the AI refusing). So each question is now asked several times per round and the typical answer is used. Extra “before” rounds are being collected while the pages are still uncrawled, purely to measure how much these answers wobble on their own. Without knowing that, we couldn't tell a real change from noise.
  • The pages might never be read. If search engines never index them, the experiment says nothing about whether crawlability matters — only that a small new site went unnoticed. That outcome will be reported as inconclusive, not as proof the idea failed.
  • Two of the Wave 2 subjects are illustrations only. Population has no control group and Immigration has just one table per side, so only the Health results carry real weight.
  • The two waves aren't directly comparable. Wave 1 was asked in Bing's dedicated AI mode and Wave 2 on the ordinary results page. Each wave is still a valid before/after test against its own starting point — and each will be re-checked on the same surface it started on — but their percentages shouldn't be stacked against each other.
  • One person is doing the grading, with no second reviewer.

The full audit, including the corrections it triggered, is in the public pre-registration. A worked example of why this matters: the Wave 2 scorecard originally counted 6 questions StatCan doesn't even publish data on as part of its total — making the AI look worse than it was. That was caught in this audit and fixed before any “after” results existed.
